Public transport usage · eight Australian capital cities

Buses, trains, trams and ferries: who came back?

Before COVID-19, Australians made about 1.8 billion trips a year on city public transport. Today the total is still a tenth short. The gap is not shared equally: some cities are busier than before COVID-19, and others have lost a fifth of their riders. The charts also look at what shapes demand: population, COVID-19, the time of day and where the network reaches.

Factor: mode

Trains and buses carry the load, and trams have grown

Over 48 years, yearly trips have grown by about half. Trains grew fastest, taking share from buses until they carried as many trips as buses by 2013. Ferries are small everywhere. COVID-19 cut trips to about half their normal level at the low point, and only ferries have fully returned to their 2018-19 level.

Factor: network and population

Every city rides differently

Sydney and Melbourne have the only large train and tram networks, so they dominate the totals. Brisbane and Perth lean on buses. Canberra and Hobart are mostly buses. Counting trips per resident shows how far apart the cities are: a Sydney resident makes nearly five times as many trips a year as someone in Hobart. The rings below also show each city's mix of modes.

Factor: COVID-19

Perth and Brisbane are back. Melbourne is not.

Compare each city with its last full year before COVID-19 (2018-19). Perth and Brisbane have passed it. Melbourne has not, because its trains and trams are still about a fifth below 2018-19 levels.

Trams and light rail are strongly up in Sydney and Canberra only because new lines opened.

Factor: population growth and the car

Even before COVID-19, Australians were taking fewer trips per person in some cities

Cities grow, so total trips can rise while each resident rides less. Trips per person have fallen since 2019 in every city, and in Canberra and Hobart they were falling long before.

The share of all distance travelled by public transport shows the same pattern against the car: it has slipped in all eight cities.

Factor: COVID-19 and counting rules

Month by month: what the state figures add

The national tables stop at June 2025. Monthly figures from Transport for NSW and the Victorian Department of Transport and Planning run to mid-2026. They show a slow climb back. Sydney buses and trains sit at roughly 80% and 90% of 2019, while ferries are above it. Melbourne trams look like they jumped in 2026, but that follows a change in how trips are counted. Treat that jump with care.

Factor: time of day

Everyone travels at the same hours

Public transport is sized for the busiest hours. On a Melbourne weekday, the four hours from 7 to 9 am and from 4 to 6 pm carry about 48% of all train travel. Weekends and public holidays have no sharp peak, only a broad plateau from mid-morning to early evening.

Canberra's buses show the same two sharp peaks, and the afternoon one starts at 3 pm, which fits school finishing times. That 3 pm spike grew from 13% to 15% of daily boardings in 2020-21 even though total boardings fell by about a third, so school trips held up better than commuting. A network built for these hours has a lot of spare room in the middle of the day.

Factor: time of day and route

Trains run towards the city in the morning and away from it in the evening.

Conclusion

What the findings show

Public transport has come back unevenly, and demand is shaped by more than how many people live in a city.

  1. The recovery is uneven. Across the eight capitals, trips are at 90% of 2018-19. Perth is at 105%, while Melbourne is at 82%, level with Darwin for the weakest.
  2. Mode matters. Trains and buses carry the load, and trams and light rail are up strongly only in Sydney and Canberra, where new lines opened.
  3. Population is not the whole story. Trips per person have fallen since 2019 in every city, and public transport's share of distance travelled has slipped in all eight against the car.
  4. Time of day drives crowding. On a Melbourne weekday, the 7 to 9 am and 4 to 6 pm peaks carry about 48% of train travel, and the most crowded trips are on the Belgrave, Pakenham and Cranbourne lines.
  5. Location matters. The busiest stations are in the centre, and Sydney's Central handles about a third more entries than Melbourne's Flinders Street.

These are associations in open data, not proof of cause. Trip counts differ by city, so city comparisons use the national tables and state figures show change within one city.

About this page

Counting differs by city. The national tables (BITRE) estimate trips on one basis for all cities, so they are used for comparisons between cities. State figures count trips differently, so they are used only to show change over time within one city. Victoria began counting tram trips with on-board sensors in January 2026, and travel became free for some trips from November 2025. Both affect recent Melbourne tram figures.

Factors are associations. The page puts demand next to population, COVID-19, time of day and network reach. It does not prove that any one of them caused a change.

Not shown. Perth stop data and Darwin, Canberra and Hobart timetable data were reduced or left out to keep the page small. Adelaide figures are touch-on counts in bands of ten, so they understate real use.
